## Intent

Evaluate the candidate's problem-solving approach when the information
needed to solve the problem is not readily available. Estimation questions
are fundamentally problem-solving questions, and the approach is the key.
The interviewer is looking for whether the candidate can impose structure,
make reasonable assumptions, and work toward a reasonable answer despite
incomplete information. The precise final number is not the main point.

## Ideal structure

1. Clarify the question
2. Catalog what you know / wish you knew
3. Make an equation
4. Think about edge cases and sources
5. Break it down
6. Review and state assumptions
7. Do the math
8. Sanity check

## Scoring dimensions

- Clarification — does the candidate identify ambiguities that could materially change the approach or result?
- Structure / decomposition — do they create an equation and break the problem into solvable components?
- Assumptions & reasoning — are assumptions clearly stated, reasonable, and explained?
- Execution — can they work through the components and arithmetic in an organized way?
- Sanity checking — do they check whether the resulting answer makes sense?

## Green flags

- clarifies meaningful ambiguity before solving rather than assuming an interpretation silently
- identifies facts already known and facts that would be useful to know
- constructs a clear equation showing how the answer can be obtained
- keeps the major parts of the equation separate rather than one unwieldy calculation
- considers relevant edge cases and alternative sources before settling on the model
- breaks difficult components into smaller pieces when useful
- makes assumptions based on intuition and prior experience, while recognizing that experience may be biased or unrepresentative
- states assumptions clearly and explains why
- uses round numbers, aims for the correct ballpark rather than absolute precision
- records intermediate calculations and assumptions
- performs a final sanity check and questions an implausible answer
- understands that being far from an eventual "correct" number is acceptable if the reasoning and assumptions are sound

## Red flags

- starts solving without first determining what the question actually asks
- ignores ambiguities that would substantially affect the approach or result
- has no equation or overall structure for reaching the answer
- tries to solve the entire problem as one large calculation instead of keeping components separate
- makes assumptions without identifying them or explaining why
- allows personal experience to become an unquestioned representation of the broader population
- does not break down difficult components when the main equation alone is insufficient
- gets overly concerned with exact numerical precision instead of targeting the right ballpark
- does not record intermediate steps or assumptions, making it hard to locate an error
- reaches an implausible result but fails to sanity check or investigate the discrepancy
- treats one sample answer as the only acceptable approach — estimation problems can have many correct approaches

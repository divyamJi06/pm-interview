## Intent

Assess business judgment on an open-ended strategy, market, or business-model
question ("If you were CEO of X...", "What's the biggest threat to Y?",
"Should this company enter market Z?"). Process over correctness: the
interviewer is not evaluating whether the conclusion matches their own —
candidates are not expected to hold the company's data — but rather how the
problem is structured, how sound the instincts are, and whether the candidate
drives the discussion forward.

## Ideal structure

1. Accept the question as fair: reason from what is known instead of pleading
   insufficient company knowledge
2. Break the strategy into components and tackle each in a structured way;
   segment the market or divide the strategy rather than treating it as one
   blob
3. Think at two levels — the micro level (is there a viable business model,
   what steps succeed on it, will customers want it) and the macro level (how
   does this fit the company's greater vision: does it open up new
   opportunities or secure an existing market)
4. Use a framework only as a scaffold for the analysis — SWOT, the Five Cs,
   Porter's 5 Forces, the Marketing Mix — or better, build an original
   framework blended from what actually fits the question
5. Weigh candidate strategies and their tradeoffs (benefits vs. downsides,
   short- vs. long-term): diversifying revenue, building barriers to entry,
   being the one-stop shop, low-cost leadership, reducing reliance on a key
   buyer or supplier, testing a new market
6. Make the best decision possible and wrap up with a clear, succinct
   conclusion of the reasoning

## Scoring dimensions

- Process over correctness — graded on the reasoning, not the final answer
- Problem structure — breaks an open-ended question into components
- Business instincts — makes sensible business decisions with incomplete data
- Strategy depth — covers both the micro (business model) and macro (company
  vision, market position) levels
- Framework judgment — uses a framework naturally or builds an original one;
  never regurgitates or force-fits
- Drive — gives an exhaustive, reasoned response rather than short answers
  that let the interviewer pull
- Question judgment — asks useful questions but knows when to stop and take
  the driver's seat
- Conclusion — commits to a position and explains why

## Green flags

- treats an "unfair" or knowledge-heavy question as fair and reasons anyway
- segments the market or divides the strategy into components
- covers the micro level (business model, will customers want it) and the
  macro level (company vision, opportunity, defensibility)
- applies a framework naturally to the problem, or creates/blends one
- names the company's genuine strengths, weaknesses, and threats
- weighs benefits and tradeoffs, short- vs. long-term
- backs every position with a reason
- commits to a decision and closes with a clear, succinct conclusion
- asks a few sharp clarifying questions, then gets back in the driver's seat

## Red flags

- dismisses the question as unfair or insists on not knowing the company well
  enough
- reels off memorized frameworks without applying them to this problem
- force-fits a framework onto the question
- analyzes at only one level — the product OR the company strategy, not both
- rides the interviewer: every prompt draws a short, thin response
- asks so many questions the interviewer flips it back ("well, what do you
  think?")
- never commits to a decision or offers a conclusion
- makes claims without reasoning and ignores tradeoffs or threats
